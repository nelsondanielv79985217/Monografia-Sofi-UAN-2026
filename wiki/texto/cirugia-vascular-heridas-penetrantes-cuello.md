Vascular surgery for penetrating injury of the neck*
T. U. Cohnert, S. Koter, S. Schweiger, J. Fruhmann, P. Konstantiniuk, A. Baumann
Division of Vascular Surgery, Department of Surgery, Medical University Graz, Graz, Austria
Received September 19, 2011; accepted September 22, 2011

Summary. Background: Vascular injuries due to penetrating neck trauma are rare, but potentially lethal
injuries.
Methods: 17 patients were treated for vascular injuries after penetrating neck trauma by specialised vascular
surgeons between 1.1.1998 and 31.12.2010. All patients
underwent surgical exploration and vascular surgical
treatment for bleeding.
Results: Trauma mechanism was stab wound in 9
patients, metal fragments in 3 and various items in 5
patients. 4 patients had attempted suicide, and 3 patients
were injured in a fight or murder attempt. The injuries
were to zone II according to Monson in 14 patients, zone I
in 2 patients, and zones I and II in 1 patient. In 8 patients
arterial injuries and in 9 patients venous lesions were
treated. Mortality was 11.8% (2/17 patients).
Conclusions: Penetrating trauma to the neck is a
serious and potentially lethal injury. CTA is recommended in the literature in diagnostic exploration of
hemodynamically stable patients. Emergency surgery by
an experienced vascular surgeon can help to determine
the extent of trauma and its treatment. In arterial trauma
to the carotid arteries a complete intraoperative exposure
with external and internal inspection of the vessel including the intimal layer is recommended. The role of endovascular treatment for vascular injury due to
penetrating neck trauma needs further evaluation.
Keywords: Penetrating neck trauma, vascular injury,
vascular reconstruction.

Patients and methods
In a specialised vascular surgery database patient and
vascular surgery data were collected prospectively. Retrospectively the records of all patients undergoing vascular surgery for non-iatrogenic traumatic lesions between
1.1.1998 and 31.12.2010 were analyzed. An experienced
vascular surgeon is always in-hospital and present at first
admission of any major trauma patient in our hospital.
Data regarding patient demographics, mechanism
of injury and clinical presentation were retrieved from the
database and entered in a new Excel file. Operation notes
as well as photographs documented the vascular lesion,
method of repair and associated injuries. Initial management and resuscitation were according to standard
advanced trauma and life support (ATLS+) guidelines.
Operative repair was according to vascular surgical emergency standards [2].
Results of the retrospective analysis are presented
as mean±standard error of the mean (SEM). Descriptive
analysis was performed for clinical and demographic
data. Patients treated for iatrogenic vascular injuries were
excluded from the analysis. Additionally patients operated by other departments for penetrating neck lesions
without vascular trauma are not included in this report.

31.12.2010. Surgery for vascular lesions due to penetrating trauma of the neck was performed in 17 patients (17/
80 ¼ 31.3%) between 20 and 79 years with a mean age of
45.8 ± 19.6 years. Two patients of the 14 male and 3 female
patients were polytraumatized.

Discussion
The optimal diagnostic and therapeutic approaches in
the management of penetrating neck injuries have undergone considerable debate over the last years. As a
vascular surgery unit we cannot give data on non-surgical
treatment. Therefore, this report describes our operative
results in comparison to the literature.
The trauma mechanism is important for the diagnostic and therapeutic strategy. In different patient age
groups and geographical locations the trauma is caused
by very different entities. In pediatric patients in Florida
according to Abujamra the major cause of injury are
motor vehicle accidents followed by gunshot wounds and
animal bites [4]. Additionally there are major differences
on trauma extent depending on a civilian or military
background of the injured patient because of the energy
transmitted by the weapon or missile. Kesser reported
that stabbings have lower surrounding tissue injury than
small calibre gunshot wounds which have lower tissue
damage than high-powered rifle or shotgun blasts [5]. In
contrast to the literature we saw no injuries to the neck
from penetrating missiles, neither low-velocity nor highvelocity shots as described in 26% of patients by Bagheri

were taken directly to the operating room. 90% of these
patients had either major arterial or venous injuries
requiring repair [12]. According to Kesser’s review
zone II injuries are typically explored whereas the
surgical complexity in approaching zones I and III has
sparked a more selective approach to dealing with the
injuries [5].
Both mandatory surgical exploration and selective
approaches to the evaluation of patients with penetrating
neck trauma have strong supporters in the literature. In
diagnostic modalities the traditionally used invasive arterial angiography has been compared to high quality CT
angiogram (CTA) with the advantage of the less invasivity
in CTA and similar accuracy in diagnosis [13]. CTA provides information about the vascular structures as well as
the soft tissue of the neck in a rapid way [14]. In recent
studies the use of CTA could reduce the number of
surgical neck explorations [11, 15]. Observational treatment as reported by Inaba for 8 hemodynamically stable
patients with injury to the internal jugular vein out of 63
penetrating neck injuries has not been our treatment
policy [16]. In all our patients active bleeding was indication for immediate surgery. 12 of 17 patients did not
undergo diagnostic procedures. Bell who reported a large
patient group and recommended selective surgical intervention stated as indications for immediate surgical
intervention exsanguinating hemorrhage, expanding hematoma, shock, airway compromise and massive subcutaneous emphysema [10]. In our patient group we saw a
man with a minor lesion to the thyroid artery leading to a
massive hematoma with airway shift who needed bleeding control as well as removal of the hematoma (Fig. 4). In
summary the decision between primarily surgical and a
selective/observational approach remains highly controversial in the literature [17, 18].
In operative treatment there are also controversial
issues. A recommendation is given by Maier for patients
with injuries to the common or internal carotid artery
to treat the arterial lesion by direct suture [19]. We lost a
43-year-old man because of carotid surgery without exploration of the intimal layer of the internal carotid artery
due to postoperative carotid occlusion and fatal hemi-

spheric stroke. In patients with zone II injuries and stable
cardiopulmonary function we now prefer complete
exploration of the internal and distal common carotid
artery including placement of a temporary intraluminal
shunt to either safely exclude of to treat intimal lesions.
In the recent literature endovascular treatment of
vascular injuries is discussed often. Interventional endoluminal treatment for vascular injuries was primarily
applied to elective procedures treating posttraumatic
pseudoaneurysms and occlusion of arteriovenous fistulas
[20, 21]. Recently endovascular procedures were performed in emergency situations and even in hemodynamically unstable patients [22, 23]. After failed surgery
with soft tissue trauma that did not allow complete
vascular reconstruction or bleeding control endovascular
treatment is a valid option [10, 24]. The still evolving
endovascular technology may be of advantage for the
patient in selected cases, but at present has little role for
more significant injuries [1]. In our hospital the interventional emergency treatment is a difficult approach because an experienced interventional radiologist is not
always in the hospital as opposed to the Board certified
vascular surgery specialist on in-hospital call 24/7.
The mortality in our patient group with 11.8% is
about as high is in comparable European centers with
12.5% in the higher range compared to large US-American trauma centers with 2.7% mortality in 223 patients in
Los Angeles, 2.5% in Bronx/N.Y. [7, 8, 25]. A possible
explanation for this is the fact that arterial injuries contribute substantially to negative outcomes in penetrating
neck trauma, especially when the carotid arteries are
involved [14, 26]. In our patients the percentage of arterial
injuries with 47.6% was higher compared to 20% arterial
lesions in Bell’s patients [10]. Especially our patient who
died of fatal ipsilateral stroke after staple-gun injury to the
internal carotid artery might have been saved by an even
more aggressive vascular surgical approach with exploration of the carotid after arteriotomy and inseption of
the intimal layer. Additionally the majority of our patients
(9/17 ¼ 53%) were transported to this tertiary care center
by helicopter. A pre-hospital patient selection of more
severely injured patients can have contributed additionally to the relatively high mortality. To improve patient
care and treatment results in trauma victims currently a
new trauma center is in the planning phase offering
optimal diagnostic facilities and hybrid structures to
prevent transportation times between ER, diagnostic facilities and operating suites.
