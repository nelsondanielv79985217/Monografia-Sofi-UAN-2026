Objective: To describe the epidemiology and patterns of gymnastics-related Head & Neck trauma injuries using
the NEISS database from 2001 to 2020.
Study design and setting: Cross-sectional analysis of a national database.
Methods: Gymnastics-related ED visits between 2001 and 2020 were queried from the NEISS database. Bivariate
chi-squared analyses were used to compare injury demographics, location, type, and disposition. Fracture
location was identiﬁed using the narrative description of each case and were divided into subtypes for further
analysis.
Results: 1455 gymnastics-related head and neck traumatic injuries were identiﬁed. The majority were in females
(65.8%). The most common presenting age group was pediatric (≤18 years) (92.7%), and the largest racial group
was Caucasian (51.5%). Of all location subtypes, facial injuries were the most common presenting injury type
overall (45.2%). Regarding injury types, lacerations were most common (36.8%), followed by dental injury
(30.7%) and fractures (21.2%). The most common location of head and neck fractures was the nose (45.8%),
followed by cervical spine (16.7%) and orbit (13.3%). The majority (95.7%) of gymnastics-related head and
neck traumatic injuries presenting to the ED were treated and discharged.
Conclusion: This study characterizes gymnastics-related head and neck injuries which is a topic that is understudied. The ﬁndings from this study are helpful for gymnasts and those who care for them including providers,
coaches and guardians, and this data may help inform future guidelines for treatment and injury prevention.

1. Introduction
Artistic gymnastics is a physically demanding sport, with both female and male gymnasts competing on a diverse set of events.
Women compete on vault, bars, beam and ﬂoor [1], while men compete
on ﬂoor, pommel horse, rings, vault, parallel bars, and the horizontal bar
[2]. Successful participation in these events requires a combination of
coordination, strength, power, speed, and ﬂexibility [3,4]. Gymnastics
is a challenging sport that carries a substantial injury risk. For instance,
the window for correct release parameters and successful dismount
from the men's high bar has been reported to be ∼73–84 milliseconds
[5]. Another more well-known example is the phenomenon of losing
air space awareness while twisting (colloquially referred to as “the

2. Methods
An analysis of the Gymnastics-related head and neck trauma was
conducted using the NEISS database. Maintained by the Consumer Product Safety Commission (CPSC) since the year 2000, NEISS is comprised
of data on consumer product-related injuries collected retrospectively
from the emergency departments of 100 hospitals around the United
States selected as a probability sample of all 5000+ U.S. hospitals with
emergency departments. This database provides pertinent demographics (age, sex, and race), injury information (location, type, and disposition), and a short descriptive narrative of each emergency department
(ED) presentation.
Data from the NEISS databases from 2001 to 2020 were combined.
Eligibility criteria for this study were gymnastics-related cases of all
ages of head and neck traumatic injuries (laceration, fracture, concussion, contusion/abrasion, and dental injury). These cases were identiﬁed
by using the Product, Body Part and Diagnosis variables within the database. The locations of fracture were identiﬁed using the narrative description of each case and were divided into subtypes (nasal,
mandibular, midface, cervical spine and skull) for further analysis. National estimates were calculated using the weight variable in the
NEISS database.
SPSS v27 (IBM, Armonk, NY) and GraphPad Prism 9 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA) software were used for data analysis and data visualization, respectively. Bivariate chi-squared analyses were
performed to compare categorical variables. Statistical signiﬁcance
was deﬁned as p < 0.05. Because this study utilized a publicly available
database maintaining anonymity, it did not require institutional review
board approval.

4. Discussion
Although head and neck injuries occur less commonly in gymnastics
than in contact sports [4], these injuries can have substantial consequences. Despite having the potential to cause a major impact on quality
of life, gymnastics injuries are generally under-characterized in the literature. This study raises awareness about the prevalence of head and
neck injuries among gymnasts.
Our ﬁndings showed that injuries to the head represent over one
third of head and neck gymnastics-related injuries, with concussions
as the most common injury type. TBI and its related syndromes
(e.g., chronic traumatic encephalopathy) are becoming a greater part
of the public health discourse around sports [17]. Although USA Gymnastics has published a concussion protocol document [18], and there
are several published return-to-play guidelines [19], such protocols
are not always followed by athletes and/or their coaching staff [19]. Interestingly, given that gymnastics-related injuries are most frequently
seen in the pediatric population, organizational and guardian involvement in enforcing concussion protocols warrant a great deal of scrutiny.

It is reassuring that only a small number of patients presenting with
head and neck injuries due to gymnastics require admission for further
management. However, there remain a high proportion of fracture
types, such as cervical spine (16.7%) and orbital (13.3%) that have the
potential for catastrophic injury and the need of specialist care [20,21].
Interestingly, it has been shown previously that females who specialized early in their athletic careers—as is common in gymnastics—were
at an overall increased risk for injury [22]. It is imperative that proper
safety precautions are in place for all skill levels, to minimize severe
injury types.
This study is not without limitations. One of these main limitations is
the retrospective nature of the NEISS database and its reporting biases.
While NEISS compiles reports from a nationally representative probability sample of hospitals [12], it is not exhaustive. Unreported injuries
could have different characteristics and frequencies seen in the NEISS
database. Furthermore, the database only includes data on emergency
department visits, rather than hospital admissions, managements such
as surgical interventions or comorbidities of presenting cases. Thus, it
might over- or under-estimate particular injury types. It also cannot
be used to comment on differences in overall management strategies
or outcomes, which would add important and clinically relevant granularity to the data.

5. Conclusion
This study characterizes gymnastics-related head and neck injuries
as reported to the NEISS database between 2001 and 2020. These injuries are most commonly seen in the pediatric population. Concussions
are the most frequent type of head injuries, and lacerations were the
most common overall injury type. Information on gymnastics-related
head and neck injuries from this study can be used to improve the
care of gymnasts with head and neck injuries, devise strategies for injury prevention, and to promote proper recovery and safe return to
sport.
