Trauma to the neck poses a unique set of challenges owing to the presence of multiple important
structures lying in close proximity to each other. A thorough understanding of the anatomy of the
neck as well as having a step wise approach for timely evaluation and management, are crucial in
identifying and managing neck injuries. Trauma to the neck can occur due to blunt or penetrating
mechanisms. Though the injury patterns may differ, the initial management is similar and follows an
algorithm based approach based on anatomic landmarks as well as patient stability. Various imaging
and diagnostic modalities are available and are utilized in the prompt diagnosis of cerebrovascular
and aerodigestive neck injuries.

Introduction
When considering the management of neck trauma, the
most critical factor is whether the mechanism is blunt or
penetrating. Blunt trauma comprises the majority of neck
injuries and includes motor vehicle collisions, falls, and
sport injuries; hanging and strangulation comprise a special
subset. The cervical spine and the laryngotracheal airway
are most commonly injured in blunt trauma.1 Additionally,
blunt cerebrovascular injury (BCVI) may occur and can be
life threatening. Penetrating trauma represents 5%-10% of
all neck trauma and describes injury that has breached the
platysma muscle.2 Stab and gunshot wounds constitute the
majority of such injuries, with gunshot wounds causing
more significant injury to a larger number of structures
in the neck and overall requiring more frequent operative
intervention.3

Penetrating trauma to the neck can cause injuries to
multiple organ systems: Vascular (occlusion, dissection, extravasation, or fistula formation); aerodigestive (which are
associated with high morbidity and mortality especially if
diagnosis and treatment are delayed); neurologic (from injuries to the spinal cord, brachial plexus, and cranial nerves
VII-XII); and injury to the upper thorax (leading to hemoor pneumothoraces).2

Anatomy

been divided into 3 zones extending from caudal to cranial,

II continues from the cricoid cartilage to the angle of the
mandible. Zone III includes the angle of the mandible to
the base of the skull. Additionally, the posterior border
