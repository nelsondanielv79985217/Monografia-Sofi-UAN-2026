Abstract
The neck is a close compartment wherein many vital structures like carotid and vertebral vessels, larynx, trachea, pharynx,
oesophagus and spinal cord are packed closely. As a result penetrating neck injury with either sharp objects like knife or
projectile injury can result in significant morbidity and mortality. There have been considerable changes in management
strategies of penetrating neck injuries time to time with most of injuries managed conservatively in hemodynamically
stable patients. CT angiography plays an important role in detecting injuries to structure which require immediate surgery/
endovascular intervention and helps to avoid unnecessary surgical exploration in stable patients. This article intends to
familiarize radiologists with imaging features of injuries to various structures of neck which shouldn’t be missed and
highlight stepwise approach for evaluation of neck structures in penetrating injury.

Evolution of management of penetrating
neck injuries
Earlier, from the era of civilian war to World War II, most of
the penetrating neck injuries were managed conservatively
[7–10]. However, by the end of World War II, it was realised
that penetrating neck injuries resulted in significant morbidity and mortality mainly due to stroke and neuromuscular
compromise which are missed on physical examination
[6, 11–17]. After World War II to last few decades, almost
all penetrating neck injuries with breach to platysma were
managed with surgical exploration [6, 15, 18–22]. Most of
the explorations did not yield positive findings and it was
realised most patients can be managed conservatively [3–5,
20].
Later, zone based approach for management was developed based on which injuries were managed based on zone
of entry wound and depth of penetration. Neck was divided
into 3 zones. The boundaries and important contents of various zones are described in Table 1.
According to this approach, all injuries in zone II underwent surgical exploration. Zone I and zone III were difficult to access by surgery and surgery was performed in
only selected cases and injuries in these zones were evaluated by different modalities like conventional angiography,
esophagography, endoscopy, ultrasound and other modalities [18–22]. MDCT angiography is a recently developed
investigation for evaluation of PNI and is fast, accurate and
reliable technique in excluding injuries requiring prompt
surgical exploration or endovascular intervention [6, 10, 13,
15, 16, 23, 24].
Recently, penetrating neck injuries are managed based
on clinical findings. Hard and soft signs predicting injuries
to various vital structures are shown in Table 2. According to this approach, patients with hard signs on physical
examination should undergo immediate surgery without
need for imaging [2, 25–27]. While patients with soft signs
should undergo MDCT angiography, further management
of these patients depends on MDCT angiography findings.

Mechanism of injury

Evaluation of wound trajectory

Penetrating injuries are classified as either low energy or
high energy injuries. Low energy injuries include low velocity projectile injuries like handgun ammunition and sharp
objects like knives, nail, etc. High energy injuries include
high velocity bullet injury from military rifles.
Injuries due to projectile are most commonly due to
stretching and crush injury [33–35]. Harvey and Korr [35]
described two types of tissue damaging pressure waves produced by projectiles such as bullets: First, sonic shock wave
is produced before the projectile hits the target. This sonic
shock wave travels with speed of sound and produces no
cavitation. Secondary pressure wave is produced when projectile hits the target, deforms tissue and radiates laterally
leading to transfer of kinetic energy to surrounding tissues
and cavitation.
The bullet may fragment into small pieces or cause bone
fragmentation. These bone and bullet fragments can serve
as secondary projectiles and produce further damage [36].
The severity of injuries caused by high velocity bullets
depend on degree of deformation of target tissues, fragmentation of bullet, velocity and size of of projectile [33].
According to Hague convention of 1899, most military rifle
bullets are fully encased in metal jacket which decreases
its deformation and fragmentation capacity, such weapons
are designed to wound the target and not kill them. Most of
ammunition used in homicide do not obey Hague rules and
produce greater injury [33, 35].
Pellets from shotguns are usually smaller in size and
have low velocity and hence when shot transfer almost
entire kinetic injury to surrounding tissues and may not exit,
leading to higher tissue damage. Severity of injury depends
on distance from the target, number of pellets that hit the
target, size and pattern of the pellets that strike the target.

The zone of injury depends on site of entry wound [7, 8,
37]. However, track of wound may traverse from one zone
to other and determining trajectory of penetrating injuries
is important to detect potential structures likely injured, so
that appropriate further investigations and management can
be done [13, 36, 38–40].
In high velocity projectile injuries, the track can be traced
by bullet or beam hardening artefacts that occur in vicinity
of bullet in the absence of deviation of bullet path.
On striking bone, the bullet may fragment into multiple
pieces and also fracture bones resulting in multiple bullet/
bone fragments which can serve as secondary projectiles.
Usually, these traverse in the same direction as bullet, however sometimes they may deviate from original path into
various directions causing injury to surrounding structures
(36). In such cases, trajectory of individual fragment needs
to be traced (Fig. 1).
In low velocity injuries by handgun, tracing track is difficult [41] as entry wound may not have exit wound and
smaller diameter of track.
In case of neck injury by sharp objects like knives, trajectory can be known by direct evidence of weapon left in situ
which is not encountered in most circumstances. In such
cases, trajectory can be estimated by the presence of air foci
and areas of haemorrhage within the tract. Alternatively,
a straight line can be drawn connecting the two structures
showing direct evidence of injury [38].

Arterial injuries
Cervical arterial injuries occur in ~ 15–25% of patients [16,
19, 23]. Injuries to carotid arteries are twice as common as
vertebral arteries with carotid vessels involved in 80% of
cases and vertebral arteries in 43% of cases [38]. Carotid
injuries are devastating because of increased incidence of
stroke in 15% cases and death in 22% cases [16, 17, 42].
Direct signs of vascular injuries include partial or complete thrombosis (Fig. 2), intimal injury, dissection, pseudoaneurysm (Fig. 3), arteriovenous fistula, extravasation of
contrast (Figs. 4 and 5) and transection [38]. Although direct
injuries may not be visible on CT angiography, arterial injuries should be suspected in presence of periarterial hematoma/ fat stranding and carotid arteries lying along course
of wound track or bone/ missile fragments/ air foci within
5 mm of arteries [38].
In patients with normal CT findings and high clinical
suspicion of vascular injury or equivocal CT findings, conventional angiography may be indicated to exclude vascular
injuries [38]. Management of patients with vascular thrombosis depends on neurological status and imaging findings
of brain. For example, in patients diagnosed with a carotid
artery occlusion, a significant neurological deficit, and a
large cerebral infarct on the ipsilateral side, observation is
the preferred treatment of choice due to the poor prognosis in this subgroup of patients. Similarly, in patients with
carotid artery occlusion and a normal neurological examination, observation and anticoagulation therapy for a period of
at least 3 months is recommended to avoid any propagation
of existent thrombus.
Minimal vascular injuries do not require as aggressive
surgical approach. Minimal injuries can be described as
nonobstructive or adherent intimal flaps and pseudoaneurysms less than 5 mm in size. Arterial repair is always preferred over ligation of carotid arteries [43, 44]. Repair can
be performed by lateral suture patch angioplasty, end to end
anastomosis, interposition or bypass grafting. Endovascular
approach with covered stents can be used for treatment of
small arteriovenous fisutulas, active contrast extravasation
and pseudo aneurysms.
Single vertebral artery injuries rarely lead to neurological
deficits and can be managed conservatively or by endovascular approach. Surgery is performed only in cases where

Venous injuries
Injuries to internal and external jugular veins are usually
missed on physical examination and are clinically insignificant. Venous injuries occur in ~ 16–18% cases with penetrating neck trauma [18, 28, 45] and when present should be
mentioned in report. Direct signs of injury include thrombosis, intimal injury and focal defect with active contrast
extravasation. The low-pressure venous system usually
tamponades or occludes without a major hemorrhage or
hematoma [46]. Venous injuries are usually detected when
surgical exploration is performed for repairing other vital
structures. An injury to the internal jugular vein should be
repaired by a lateral venorrhaphy [47]. However, when the
patient is in shock or repair is difficult, any venous injury
should be managed by ligation. The external jugular vein
can be ligated without adverse sequelae.
Aerodigestive tract

Larynx
Injuries to larynx should be reported in terms of location
(supraglottic, glottic or subglottic), structures injured (cartilage, mucosa, ligaments, joints, nerves) and severity of
injury. Whenever defect in laryngeal cartilage is detected
(Figs. 6 and 7), displaced of cartilages and airway compromise needs to be reported [7, 31, 48–50]. Undisplaced stable
fracture of cartilage is usually managed consevatively while
displaced cartilage fracture requires surgical management
[51]. Status of vocal cords must be reported to look for signs
of avulsion or recurrent laryngeal nerve injury. Many times,
defect in walls of larynx obscured by surrounding hematoma. In such cases, laryngoscopy needs to be performed
to exclude injury when larynx lies along course of wound
track.

Trachea
Tracheolaryngeal injuries occur in 1–7% of patients with
penetrating neck trauma [40, 48, 52]. Direct signs of defect
in tracheal wall are not often detected on imaging. Tracheal
injury should be suspected in cases with extensive subcutaneous emphysema. When trachea lies along course of
wound track, endoscopy should be done to exclude tracheobronchial injury [3, 10, 12, 40]. Complete or incomplete
laceration of tracheal wall needs to be reported as incomplete laceration of trachea with opposed ends are usually

Pharynx and oesophagus
Esophageal injuries are very rare and occur in ~ 0.9–6.6%
cases of penetrating neck trauma [7, 40, 53–55]. Most common cause of death in patients with esophageal injuries
include delayed detecting leading to mediastinitis and sepsis [38]. Hence, injuries involving pharynx and oesophagus

need to diagnosed and treated immediately. Injuries to pharynx and oesophagus should be suspected when wound track
appears to traverse along course of oesophagus (Fig. 8),
bone fragments/metal fragments lie close to these structures,
surrounding hematoma/ air foci in vicinity of oesophagus
are present. When these signs are present on CT angiography, conventional/ CT esophagography should be done
to rule out esophageal injuries. In patients with equivocal
findings and suspected pharyngeal injuries, endoscopy must
be performed as pharyngeal injuries are usually missed on
esophagography. Combined sensitivity of esophagography

Cervical spine and spinal cord
Cervical spine injuries occur in 11–14% cases of penetrating neck trauma [56–59]. High velocity projectiles like
bullets from military rifles cause fracture of vertebrae with
fragmentation of bones. These bone and bullet fragments
can travel along the same direction as bullet or deviate
in different directions. Low velocity injuries from sharp
objects usually result in fracture of vertebrae without bone
fragmentation.

Conclusion
Penetrating neck injuries are major cause of morbidity
and mortality in trauma settings as many vital structures
are packed closely in small compartment. CT angiography
plays a vital role in detecting injuries requiring immediate
treatment and avoids unnecessary surgical exploration in
stable patients. Timely detection of injuries helps in early
treatment and reduction of both morbidity and mortality
associated with penetrating neck trauma.
