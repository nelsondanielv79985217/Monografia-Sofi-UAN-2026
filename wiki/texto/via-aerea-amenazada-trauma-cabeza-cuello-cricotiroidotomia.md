Introduction: Trauma to the head and neck results in acute facial trauma and swelling, which may occlude the
airway and result in fatal hypoxia. The management is the establishment of a definitive airway. This paper re­
views our experience with this clinical scenario.
Materials & Methods: A retrospective study was conducted over a seven-year period from December 2012 to
January 2020 at a major trauma centre in South Africa on all adult trauma patients who required a surgical
airway procedure (surgical cricothyroidotomy (SC) or surgical tracheostomy (ST)).
Results: From December 2012 to January 2020, 12243 trauma patients were admitted. Of those, 242 patients
required a surgical airway: 98% (238/242) underwent ST and 2% (4/242) underwent SC. A total of 3271 patients
sustained trauma predominantly confined to the face and neck. The mechanism was penetrating trauma in 1077
and blunt trauma in 2194. Total of 51 patients required an emergency airway for acute head and neck trauma.
There were four SCs and 47 STs. Of the four SC, three were inserted secondary to multiple failed attempts at
intubation and one was inserted directly into an open tracheal injury. All of these were performed in the
resuscitation room. The 47 STs were performed under conscious sedation in the operating theatre. There were
eight failed intubations in the 51 patients with head and neck trauma, and of those three required an emergency
SC and five required an emergency ST.
Conclusion: The use of cricothyroidotomy in modern trauma care in our environment appears to be very limited.

1. Introduction
Trauma to the head and neck may result in acute facial trauma and
swelling, which may occlude the airway and result in potentially fatal
hypoxia. The management of this situation is the establishment of a
definitive airway. The Advanced Trauma Life Support (ATLS) course
teaches that the initial approach is endotracheal intubation. If this fails,
emergency surgical cricothyroidotomy (SC) is the salvage procedure of
choice. Surgical tracheostomy (ST) is not taught in the ATLS course. It is
not undertaken by non-surgeons and is usually performed in the oper­
ating room. Percutaneous tracheostomy is not used in the management
of acute head and neck trauma [2]. Over the last three decades, difficult

airway techniques have advanced and adjuncts such as fibre-optic
intubation, video-laryngoscopy have become widespread. There can
be no doubt that these advances have reduced the rate of failed intu­
bation and have to a large extent, drastically reduced the need for
emergency surgical airway procedures [3].
The objective of this study was to review our experience with
emergency surgical airway procedures for trauma in our environment.
We hoped to contextualise this experience and to extract objective les­
sons on which to base future training and educational initiatives.

2.1. The study
All patients admitted to our trauma centre from December 2012 to
January 2020 were reviewed. Patients who required a surgical airway
procedure during their stay were identified and extracted for review.
These include both SC and ST procedures. At our institution, all surgical
airway procedures were performed by general surgeons within the
trauma service. Only SCs or STs were performed. In this study, we define
an emergency surgical airway as one which needs to be performed either
immediately in the resuscitation room in response to a ‘can’t intubate,
can’t ventilate’ situation, or urgently within two hours of arrival in the
operating room. A non-emergency surgical airway refers to an elective
ST performed for any indication, such as to facilitate ventilator weaning.

4. Discussion
During the acute care of a patient with head and neck trauma the
management of the airway is central. Ongoing swelling and bleeding
may occlude the airway and this can be rapidly fatal. The solution is the
establishment of a definitive airway. Usually this is achieved by intu­
bating the patient. However, the trauma and swelling of the airway may
make it difficult to do this. If the clinical scenario arises where a patient
with head and neck trauma cannot be safely intubated, ATLS teaches
that SC is a safe and effective measure to achieve a temporary airway in
the acute setting [1]. This can then be converted to a definitive airway
under a more controlled setting. The ubiquity of modern intubation
techniques and video-laryngoscopes means that it is increasingly un­
usual to encounter a clinical situation in which intubation is unsuc­
cessful. In our series a surgical airway was only needed in 51 cases out of
3271 patients (1.6%) with trauma to the head and neck. When the need
arises for a surgical airway, the use of SC is also relatively rare in our
environment. Out of 51 patients with acute head and neck trauma, in
whom there was an inability to achieve intubation only four underwent
SC. The remainder underwent formal tracheostomy under a controlled
setting in the OR.
Surgical tracheostomy insertion in head and neck trauma will often
obtain reliable airway control. In a large series of 239 severe maxillo­
facial injuries in a military treatment facility, there was a significant
relationship between the presence of maxillofacial fracture and the need
for tracheostomy (p = 0.0001) [4]. There are however no large case
series exploring patient presentation with head and neck trauma and the
subsequent insertion of a SC, albeit a few case reports. This suggests that
SC is performed relatively rarely around the world for patients with head
and neck trauma.
Most of the algorithms for airway management in trauma, are nonspecific about what form of surgical airway is needed. SC is described
as being safe and easy to perform by non-surgeons [1]. However, the
current literature suggests there is a general paucity of skills in estab­
lishing a surgical airway, across the board. A six-year review from a level
one trauma centre in Washington DC noted that only 34 emergency
surgical airway procedures (24 STs and 10 SCs) were performed during
that time [5]. Further in another study conducted at a level I trauma
centre, 57 patients had a penetrating laryngotracheal injury, however 15
had a tracheostomy and only 3 patients had a cricothyroidotomy [6].
These findings are consistent with studies from other major trauma
centres [7]. If the surgical skill is performed very infrequently then it is

3. Results
During the period from December 2012 to January 2020, a total of
12243 trauma patients were admitted to our service. Out of this total,
242 patients required a surgical airway of which, 98% (238/242) un­
derwent ST and the remaining 2% (4/242) underwent SC. There were
206 male patients (85%: 206/242). The mean age was 34 years (range
0 – 85). The median Injury Severity Score (ISS) was 17. There were 125
patients who had an ISS greater than 15. A total of 3271 patients sus­
tained trauma predominantly confined to the face and neck. The
mechanism was penetrating trauma in 1077 and blunt trauma in 2194. A
total of 51 patients required an emergency airway for acute head and
neck trauma. There were four SCs and 47 STs. Of the four SC, three were
inserted secondary to multiple failed attempts at intubation and one was
inserted directly into an open tracheal injury. All of these were per­
formed in the resuscitation room. The 47 STs were performed under
conscious sedation in the OR. There were eight failed intubations in the
51 patients with head and neck trauma, and of those three required an
emergency SC and five required an emergency ST. Of the 238 ST, 20%
(47/238) were undertaken as an emergency procedure for acute head
and neck trauma. In the remaining 80% (191/238) of cases ST was
performed as an elective procedure. The indications for all surgical
airway procedures are summarised in Tables 1 and 2. Fig. 3 outlines the
number of surgical airways that were inserted over the seven year
period. Of the 242 surgical airway procedures (SC and ST), the proce­

performed than SCs. This increases our staff’s familiarity and confidence
with ST. Our current algorithm is presented in Fig. 4. There are several
limitations common to the retrospective nature of the study. The shortterm outcomes for these surgical airways were good with low rates of
early procedural complications. One of the limitations of our study was
that the long-term complication rates were unknown. In our environ­
ment, follow up is extremely difficult due to complex multitude of social,
logistical and resource factors.

5. Conclusion
The use of SC in modern trauma care in our environment appears to
be limited. With these low rates of procedures, it is unlikely that the skill
set for SC will be maintained.
