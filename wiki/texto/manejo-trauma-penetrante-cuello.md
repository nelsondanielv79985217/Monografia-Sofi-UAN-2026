Management of penetrating neck injuries
at a London trauma centre
Richard T. K. Siau • Andrew Moore •

Received: 18 August 2012 / Accepted: 11 December 2012 / Published online: 23 December 2012
Ó Springer-Verlag Berlin Heidelberg 2012

Abstract Penetrating neck injuries (PNIs) are uncommon
in the UK. The majority of guidelines are from the USA or
South Africa. No UK national guidelines exist. Increasing
urban violence in the UK has lead to an increase in PNIs.
There is a need to develop a PNI guideline that reflects the
pathology and experience in the UK. A retrospective
review of all PNIs managed at St George’s Hospital over
an 18 month period was undertaken. Data collected
included patient demographics, mechanism of injury,
investigations, multidisciplinary team involvement and
management. Clinical activity data was correlated to current worldwide literature and a flow-chart style clinical
guideline was produced. 25 neck wounds were managed
over an 18 month period. 68 % were male, 32 % female
with a mean age of 36 years. The mechanism of injury
included deliberate self-harm (48 %), stab wounds (32 %),
gunshot wounds (4 %), shotgun wounds (4 %) and other
accidental causes (12 %). 52 % of wounds were superficial
to platysma. 58 % of deep wounds had CT. 42 % of
patients with deep wounds also had panendoscopy. Interventional radiology was used in one case (8 %) and a
single case was managed jointly with the vascular team
(8 %). UK ENT surgeons have limited exposure to neck
trauma and dedicated head and neck out-of-hours cover is
uncommon. There is a need for UK PNI guidelines that
reflect local pathology and experience. The St George’s
PNI guideline can be used to facilitate assessment, documentation and management of a relatively infrequent
emergency presentation.

Introduction
Penetrating neck injuries (PNIs) are rare in the United
Kingdom. However, increasing urban violence has lead to
a rising incidence [1, 2]. The management of these injuries
remains controversial with most experience and the
majority of guidelines originating from North America and
South Africa. Conversely, there is a relative paucity of data
specific to penetrating neck trauma in the United Kingdom
where the social demographics and patterns of injury are
different. St George’s Hospital has recently become a
major trauma centre and thus there is increased need to
develop a PNI guideline reflecting the pathology and
experience of such injuries in the UK. A PNI management
algorithm is herein produced, based on the St George’s
Hospital experience and a review of the worldwide literature Table 1.
The management of PNIs has evolved significantly over
the last century. Trauma surgery has seen much advancement during military conflict. Mortality rates from PNIs
were 15 % in the American Civil War [3] and 11 % in
World War I [4], during which there was a tendency
towards expectant management which often resulted in
uncontrollable haemorrhage, the largest cause of death in
patients sustaining these injuries. Towards the end of World
War II, there was a shift from expectant management to
mandatory surgical exploration for all patients with penetrating neck injuries, with one series showing a mortality
rate of 7 % in patients sustaining PNIs [5]. As a result,
routine exploration of penetrating neck trauma was strongly
advised [5, 6]. In 1956, Fogelman and Stewart concluded

aggressive management philosophy was driven by the
fear of vascular injuries missed at physical examination
and consequent neurological complications, and rapidly
became the mainstay of management at most major trauma
centres.
The 1980s saw a paradigm shift from mandatory
exploration towards selective management of penetrating
neck injuries, as the high rates of negative exploration were
deemed unacceptable. An 18 year prospective study by
Biffl et al. showed that selective management of PNIs
based on anatomical zone of injury as well as clinical
findings was safe and cost-effective [8]. These findings
have been supported by many other studies [9–15], and
most trauma centres have now adopted the approach of
selective management based on a combination of clinical
examination and radiographic investigation. Over the last
decade, advances in imaging have consolidated the role of
selective management as the paradigm. In particular,
developments in multislice and helical computed tomography angiography (CTA) have provided a fast, accurate
non-invasive method of evaluating patients with penetrating neck injuries. Several studies have shown that CTA is
as useful as traditional catheter-based angiography in
evaluating vascular injuries in neck trauma whilst providing useful information about other structures which may be
injured such as the upper aerodigestive tract and cervical
spine [16–20]. Furthermore, the use of CTA can drastically
reduce rates of negative surgical exploration [20–22].

By definition, PNIs are injuries that penetrate the full
thickness of platysma. Penetrating injuries to the neck may
be classified into three zones according to location of site
of entry as described by Monson et al. [23]: zone I:
demarcated by the clavicles and the cricoid cartilage; zone
II: extending from the cricoid cartilage to the level of the
angle of the mandible; zone III: extending from the angle
of the mandible to the base of the skull.
The management of neck injuries is guided by the zones
involved, although it should be remembered that these
zones only apply to the anterior neck.
Zone II is readily surgically accessible and exploration
is favourable: this has resulted in high rates of negative
neck explorations, with figures ranging from 50-60 %
[24, 25]. Access to Zone I and zone III injuries is more
challenging and may involve opening the thoracic or
intracranial cavity, thus these injuries were commonly
evaluated with angiography, endoscopy, laryngoscopy and
oesophagography.
Major trauma relates to serious, often multiple injuries
where there is strong potential to cause death or serious
disability. It was recently identified that major trauma care
in the United Kingdom could be improved by the implementation of major trauma systems, potentially saving an
additional 450 to 600 lives per year. Major trauma systems
aim to improve patient outcomes by ensuring that appropriate specialist care is available to meet the urgent needs
of trauma patients, and that patients are transferred to the
most appropriate unit according to the severity of their
injuries. Each major trauma centre must provide 24 hour
emergency services catering for the management of serious

Methods
Discussion
St George’s Hospital is the major trauma centre for the
South West London and Surrey Trauma Network, providing 24 hour emergency surgical services across all surgical
subspecialties as well as interventional radiology services
for a population of approximately 1.3 million. All patients
presenting to St George’s Hospital between December
2009 and May 2011 with neck trauma were identified by a
search of the ICD-10-based hospital database.

Results
25 patients with neck wounds were identified. Of these,
there were 21 patients: one patient was admitted three
times and two patients were admitted twice each over
18 months. 68 % of admissions were male (n = 17) and
32 % were female (n = 8). The mean ages at admission
were 38.4 years (± 22.2) for males and 33.4 years (± 9.8)
for females. 48 % of presentations resulted from deliberate
self-harm (n = 12), 32 % from stab wounds (n = 8), 12 %
from accidents (n = 3), 4 % from gunshot wounds (n = 1)
and 4 % from shotgun wounds (n = 1).
It was found that a complete head and neck examination
was documented in 16 % of cases (n = 4). 28 % of cases
were examined by flexible fibreoptic nasendoscopy (n = 7).
48 % had regional nerve function assessed (n = 12).
The patients were haemodynamically stable in all 25
cases. 12 cases involved injuries violating platysma: 50 %
resulted from deliberate self-harm (n = 6), 33.3 % from
stab wounds (n = 4), 8.3 % from an accident (n = 1) and
8.3 % from shotgun wounds (n = 1). There were two cases
of zone I injury and eight cases of zone II injury. One case
involved both zone I and zone II and another involved
injuries across all three zones. 50 % of cases had vascular
involvement (n = 6): in four cases the damaged vessel was
a vein and in two cases an artery was damaged; the most
commonly injured blood vessel was the anterior jugular

Initial assessment
There is little dispute that an unstable patient should
undergo immediate surgical exploration [9, 12, 20]. In our
series, there were no unstable patients unresponsive to
resuscitation.
Initial assessment should follow Advanced Trauma Life
Support (ATLS) principles. Flexible fibreoptic nasendoscopy is essential for the examination of the endolarynx in
the stable patient: lacerations, haematomas, vocal cord
mobility and oedema should be identified at the time of
initial assessment [27]. The marginal mandibular, hypoglossal and accessory nerves are particularly vulnerable to
penetrating neck trauma. Careful examination and documentation of cranial nerve function is therefore particularly
important. Documentation of the fibreoptic assessment and
cranial nerve function was found to be poor in the St
George’s series. The St George’s penetrating neck injury
guideline acknowledges this by directing the clinician to
perform a full head and neck examination including
fibreoptic nasendoscopy and cranial nerve assessment
where possible.
If the platysma is intact then by definition the neck wound
is superficial. This fact is recognised by many international
management algorithms and is used to guide treatment [28,
29]. In 48 % of neck wounds presenting to St George’s
hospital, the platysma was breached. It was found that
local anaesthetic facilitated a more accurate assessment of
the wound. We recommend that the attending emergency
otolaryngology service should be involved in making an
assessment in all cases of penetrating neck injuries.

Damage to the upper aerodigestive tract occurs in over
20 % of PNIs [31]. From our data, one case (8.3 %)
involved damage to the upper aerodigestive tract. Normal
physical examination is not adequate to rule out upper
aerodigestive tract damage [32, 33], whereas CT imaging
can exclude these injuries in 90 % of cases [15]. It is
important to rapidly identify and treat oesophageal damage
in order to minimise patient morbidity following PNIs
[34–36]. A contrast swallow examination in the acute
setting may be of value [2, 37].
Whilst the evidence to rely on physical examination alone
for zone II trauma is convincing, these recommendations
generally reflect practice in high volume trauma centres in the
United States. The volume of PNIs in the United Kingdom is
relatively small and consequently trauma centres here are less
experienced than their counterparts in other areas of the world
at managing these injuries. Furthermore, dedicated head and

advantage of informing the surgeon about the presence of
vascular, upper aerodigestive tract or intrathoracic injuries
and allows them to plan their surgical approach accordingly.
This may also include the involvement of another surgical
discipline, for example cardiothoracics or vascular surgery, or
interventional radiology. The use of CTA has also been shown
to reduce the rate of negative surgical exploration [20–22]. It
is suggested therefore, that the routine use of CT imaging in
stable patients is safe and should form part of the management
algorithm for penetrating neck injuries. It is stressed however
that CTA should not be mandatory as a surgeon with the
necessary expertise would be able to explore a zone II injury
safely. This approach is increasingly supported in the literature [2, 37, 38].

Conservative management
Although the management of PNI remains controversial,
with no international consensus, there is a large volume of
evidence that supports selective conservative management
[8, 37, 38]. Patients who are ‘asymptomatic’ and have had
negative investigations can be safely managed conservatively. For example, a patient who does not not have hard
signs of vascular or upper aerodigestive tract damage such
as haematoma, ongoing haemorrhage, dysphonia, dysphagia and in whom a CTA was negative could be managed
conservatively according to the St George’s guideline. This
approach is similar to that described by Harris et al., who
conclude that selective conservative management based on
the absence of haemodynamic instability and radiological
findings reduces length of hospital stay, lightens surgical
workload and cuts costs without affecting morbidity or
mortality [2]. It is recognised that these patients who are
managed ‘conservatively’, i.e. do not have a formal neck
exploration, may still require wound revision and skin
closure.
The aetiology of PNIs in St George’s differs from that in
the USA. The largest cause found in our series is deliberate
self-harm, accounting for approximately half of the presentations. In contrast, the largest causes in the USA are
gun shot wounds and knife stab wounds [8, 39]. The
St George’s guideline acknowledges this difference by
making reference to psychiatric referral where appropriate.

presently small compared to centres in the USA and South
