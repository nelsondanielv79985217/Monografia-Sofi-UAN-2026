INTRODUCTION
Epidemiology

Penetrating neck injury (PNI) is best described as
an injury caused by a missile that penetrates the
platysma muscle from the base of the skull superiorly to the sternum inferiorly. Isolated neck injuries represent up to 10% of all trauma cases, and
mortality can be as high as 10%.1 2 Most commonly,
injuries are caused by stab wounds, although
gunshot wounds (GSWs) constitute a large plurality
of the incidence as well.3 Majorly injured structures in descending order of incidence include the
internal jugular vein, cervical spine, larynx, external
jugular, vertebral arteries, pharynx, and vagus
nerve.4 Between 35% and 50% of GSW victims will
have clinically significant injuries compared with
10%–20% of stab wound victims.5 6

Neck trauma has been of interest to trauma surgeons,
and data have been collected as early as the American Civil War, where more than 4000 cases of neck
wounds were reported with a mortality of greater
than 35% without operative intervention.7 Care of
these wounds remained primarily non-­operative in
nature, well into the 20th century.6 World War I
demonstrated a mortality of greater than 16% with
this management, and it was not until World War
II that mandatory neck exploration was instituted
among allied forces, with a subsequent reduction in
mortality to 4%–7%.8–11 However, as expected, the
negative exploration rate was extremely high, and
alternative clinical criteria were sought.11
The zones of the neck have been classically used
to assist in decision-­
making and were particularly useful in the pre-­CT era. Monson et al first
described these three zones in 1969 at the Cook
County Hospital, and this definition was further

refined by Roon and Christensen in 1979.12 13
(table 1)
After the advent of thin-­sliced, high-­quality CT
angiography imaging and improvements in accessibility, management has increasingly shifted to
patient-­specific surgical management. For example,
this approach is beginning to obviate the need for
mandatory exploration in certain zone II injuries. This ‘no zone’ approach, although becoming
popular parlance, still requires astute decision-­
making, and when the provider is in doubt, consultation with experienced trauma or vascular surgeons
should be undertaken.

ANATOMY AND CLASSICAL ZONES OF THE
NECK
Definition of the neck and divisions
The neck is bounded superiorly by the mandible
and inferiorly by the clavicles.14 There are many
important structures running through the neck,
making it of critical importance to have a complete
evaluation when injury occurs to this area. Anatomically, the neck is divided into the anterior and
posterior triangles, which both contain further
subdivisions into smaller triangles.14 The anterior triangle is further divided into the submental,
submandibular, carotid, and muscular triangles.14
The posterior triangle is divided into the occipital
triangle and the subclavian triangle.14 Although
these anatomic classifications are important and
assist in identifying critical structures, in traumatic
injuries, the zone classification systems have been
used to aid in management decisions.
There are three traditional zones of the neck
(figure 1).15 Zone I extends from the clavicles
and sternal notch to the cricoid cartilage. Zone
II is located between the cricoid cartilage and the
angle of the mandible.13 16 Zone III is the most
superior zone and extends from the angle of the
mandible to the base of the skull.13 16 These classifications have been used to try and predict what
structures are most at risk for injury based on what
they contain. Zone I contains the innominate and
subclavian vessels, carotid artery origin and vertebral artery, the brachial plexus, esophagus, trachea,
and lung apex.16 Zone II contains the carotid and
vertebral arteries, the internal jugular veins, the
esophagus, trachea, and cranial nerves X, XI, and
XII (online supplemental video 2).16 17 Zone III
contains the distal vertebral and carotid arteries,
the pharynx, as well as cranial nerves VII, IX, X,
XI, and XII16 17 (table 1). Operative exposure of the
common carotid, internal jugular, and vagus nerve
has been demonstrated in figure 2.

MANAGEMENT

Historically, injuries to the neck were managed based on zone
and mechanism of injury. Zone II injuries were all taken for
mandatory operative neck exploration whereas zones I and
III were managed based on stability and symptoms as they are
more difficult to access.16 We have since evolved this to be more
nuanced based on injury location, mechanism, patient stability,
and imaging, which we will discuss in depth in the following
sections.

Prehospital assessment and management

Prehospital care of PNIs can often be difficult, and emergency
medical service (EMS) providers should be properly educated
that management cannot be universal. Impaled objects should
not be removed.18 Patients who are adequately protecting their
airway, regardless of the subjective extent of damage, should
only be cautiously intubated. Patients with significant agitation
and hemodynamic instability should be intubated with rapid
sequence intubation (RSI), but cricoid pressure and bag valve
mask ventilation should be avoided to prevent worsening of
aerodigestive tract or great airway injuries.19 20 RSI was found
to be very effective even in the setting of PNI when performed
at the discretion of the provider.20 Although there is no evidence
for the use of video laryngoscope (VL) versus direct laryngoscope (DL) intubation in the setting of PNI, VL has been
demonstrated to have superior success rates in other prehospital
patients and intuitively should be expected to be higher quality

Initial assessment and treatment

Based on the EMS report, the trauma team and resuscitation
bay should be prepared accordingly, with intubation equipment,
blood products, foley catheters, thoracotomy, and tube thoracostomy equipment with easy access to portable X-­rays. Team roles
should be defined early, and advanced trauma life support principles should be carefully observed in the initial assessment.
Airway management can proceed similarly as in the prehospital
setting if intubation has not already occurred. Clinical signs of
impending airway collapse, such as stridor, hoarseness, bubbling
from the wound, worsening tracheal deviation, or subcutaneous
emphysema should encourage early intubation, in addition to
usual indications, such as Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score
of <8. As in the prehospital setting, VL is the preferred route,
although DL in the hands of a capable provider is also often
adequate. Cricothyroidotomy is the preferred invasive airway in
the event endotracheal tube (ETT) cannot placed. Another less
conventional option for airway management is intubation via
existing tracheal laceration. In the event of a patient presenting
with an existing injury that extends into the trachea already, it
is reasonable to place an ETT into the existing laceration as a
temporizing measure until the patient can be stabilized, critical injuries otherwise controlled, and then taken for definitive
airway repair. Extreme caution is needed due to the risk of worsening a false passage. End-­tidal monitoring is mandatory.

Additional adjuncts that are helpful include bougies and cricoid

Additionally, a consideration should be forming a dedicated
multidisciplinary team for difficult airways as seen in the dialogue
around respiratory illness treatment quality improvement initiative from Mark et al.28 They created response teams to better
identify difficult airways, ensure equipment was ready and available, and quickly deploy staff to these situations.28 Although not
all hospitals have the capacity to have specialists, having a team
of this nature can help ensure that everyone can operate at their
best level with the resources they do have.
Blind intubation should never be attempted, regardless of the
presumed urgency of obtaining the airway, and surgical airway
should be done first. Emergency tracheostomy can be performed
in cases where there is extensive damage to the cricoid cartilage or superior trachea. The emergency physician or trauma
surgeon who is not comfortable with this procedure should have
an attending otolaryngologist or other surgical subspecialist
with extensive airway experience, available as not obtaining the
airway on the first attempt may result in disastrous complications. Central line placement should not occur until the definitive airway is secured and all other options, such as large bore
upper extremity peripheral access or intraosseus placement have
been exhausted.

and should be done with thorough physical examination probing
to assess the extent of the wound to ensure that they do or do
not extend past the platysma. An example of a burn patient

DEFINITIVE MANAGEMENT

As previously mentioned, the classic zones of the neck are
still used to delineate the anatomy. This scheme provided a
framework for management decisions and ease of access to
anatomic structures, such as mandatory exploration in zone II
with platysmal violation or triple endoscopy and angiography
in zone III injuries. However, this dogma is being increasingly
challenged, particularly given the evidence that exterior wounds
correlate poorly with the exact zone of injury.22

As the neck contains several complex structures in a compact
space, such as numerous vessels, aerodigestive structures, and
various neurological structures, all neck injuries, regardless of
the caliber of the missile, should be approached with extreme
caution (box 1). Vascular injuries, particularly arterial ones,
can result in major hemorrhage, neurological deficits, or both,
particularly when multiple vessels are involved. Common injury
patterns include partial or total lacerations with or without
obstruction, pseudoaneurysm formation, dissection, or fistula
formation.29 The incidence of vascular injury is as high as 25%
and is most commonly carotid injuries, followed by vertebral
artery injury.2 Neck aerodigestive tract injuries, although less
devastating than those that occur in the mediastinum, are a large
source of morbidity and occur in up to 30% of patients with
PNIs.2 Non-­vascular neurological injuries, such as injury to the
brachial plexus, spinal cord and nerve roots, and cranial nerves,
are also a significant source of morbidity.

Non-operative management and the ‘no one’ approach

Non-­
operative management has evolved as surgeons are
becoming more aware of the non-­zero risk of non-­therapeutic
interventions.30 Prior to the evolvement of thin-­sliced helical
CTA, triple endoscopy with a traditional angiogram of the neck
vessels was considered the gold standard for non-­
operative
management. Triple endoscopy refers to performing laryngoscopy, bronchoscopy, as well as an endoscopy to fully assess for
any injuries of the aerodigestive tract due to concern for missed
injury with only one method.31 However, there are risks associated with endoscopy, including perforation and hemorrhage
and angiograms carry increased radiation-­
load risk, potential

As the quality and availability of CT scan imaging continue
to improve, ‘no-­zone’ management for hemodynamically stable
patients is rapidly becoming the standard of care in level 1
trauma centers across the USA.30 32 Rather than divide the neck
into three distinct regions, it is treated as a singular anatomic
region, and the focus is placed on the nature of specific injuries.
Multidetector CTA of the neck and chest provides excellent resolution regarding vascular, airway, and aerodigestive tract injury. The American College of Radiology cites a
90%–100% sensitivity and 98.6%–100% specificity for evaluating vascular injury with CTA and cites a 100% sensitivity
for aerodigestive injuries with 93.5%–97.5% specificity.33
Patients can therefore be safely observed if no injury is detected.
However, in patients with missile tracts near pharyngoesophageal

structures, sensitivity is reduced to as low as 53%, and therefore confirmatory studies with contrast esophagram followed
by esophagoscopy in cases of non-­diagnostic studies are mandatory.23 24 Finally, in cases of diagnostic uncertainty based on CTA,
such as ‘near-­miss’ injuries, further evaluation should occur with
traditional angiography and triple endoscopy, or single modalities depending on the area of concern. If these options are not
available, the surgeon must pursue a mandatory exploration,
being careful to rule out contralateral injury.34

study is a much lower rate of stroke with ligation, which had

Vertebral artery injuries can be very difficult to manage given
their location.36 41 V1 injuries, the traditional zone I injuries,
will require median sternotomy with control of the vessel at
its origin at the subclavian artery.36 V2 injuries pose a particular challenge.36 In the hemodynamically unstable patient or in
cases where an endovascular approach is not possible, operative
ligation can be performed by identifying the transverse process
below the injury and ligating the vessel in the transverse foramina
just medial to the TP with a permanent suture. V3/4 injuries,
zone III injuries, are generally impossible to manage operatively;
however, tamponade with bone wax or a similar substance can
be attempted by placing it into the foramina inferior to the area
of injury. Interventional radiology embolization will invariably
be required.36

Endovascular therapy is a newer field with increased use in
the traumatic setting, generally reserved for stable patients. They
can be very helpful for distal vertebral artery injuries or difficult-­
to-­access locations.37 42 However, they are generally contraindicated for carotid repairs due to the risk of thrombosis.37 These
attempts at minimally invasive intervention have been increasing;
however, further study is needed prior to widespread acceptance
for the management of zone II injuries.42
Venous injuries can generally be ligated if they are not bilateral, given the extensive network of collaterals.43 In the setting of
bilateral jugular vein injuries, either primary repair if <25% or
patch or interposition repair if >25% of the diameter of the
vessel should be attempted if the patient is hemodynamically
stable.44 In unstable cases, primary repair of a larger injury
followed by revision when the patient is stable is an acceptable
approach.44
Repair of the trachea can be very difficult for the unfamiliar
trauma surgeon, and consultation of an attending otolaryngologist, or other qualified surgical subspecialist, should be made
early if needed. Oblique lateral neck incisions or collar incisions
are both usually adequate for access, and rarely a median sternotomy may be required for intrathoracic injuries.36 Small injuries with minimal tissue loss can usually be repaired primarily
with absorbable sutures after debridement to healthy margins.36
In cases with >50% tissue loss, such as transcervical GSWs, resection of the segment and primarily anastomosis may be required.45
We recommend a vascular muscular pedicle to buttress the repair
and neck flexion be established with a guardian suture from the
chin to the chest or head cradle.46
Esophageal injuries can usually be accessed through a left
lateral oblique neck incision, although transcervical incisions are
best accessed through a collar incision.36 The surgeon should be
sure to explore the esophagus circumferentially in cases where
prior imaging was not obtained. This can be best performed with
a collar incision or endoscopy if an oblique left lateral incision
was used for a different purpose. Like tracheal injuries, clean
small injuries can be repaired primarily with absorbable sutures
after debridement, whereas >50% of injuries will require
segmental resection, anastomosis, and pediculated flaps for
protection, although with wide drainage.47
8

Prognosis/outcomes

Due to the amount of critical structures in the neck, there are a
wide variety of devastating injuries that can occur that may leave
patients with lifelong disability, such as injury to the brachial
plexus, dysphagia, vocal cord paralysis, and airway injury
requiring tracheostomy.48–50
Overall, the mortality associated with a PNI is
3%–6% accounting for approximately 3500 deaths yearly.17 48
Half of these deaths occur due to hemorrhage from vascular
injuries, and 10% of PNIs have been reported to have carotid
artery injuries.17 48 51 Of those with vascular injury, 40% will
develop complications.17 Of those with aerodigestive injuries,
the mortality rate is 11%–17%.17 Aerodigestive injuries occur in
23%–30% of injuries.17 48
Given their relatively high prevalence of traumatic injuries and
mortality rate, we need to continue to improve our management
strategies of PNIs with particular emphasis on hemorrhage control.
