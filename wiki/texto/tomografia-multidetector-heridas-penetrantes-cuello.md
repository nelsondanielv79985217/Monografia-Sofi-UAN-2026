Multidetector Row Computed Tomography
in the Management of Penetrating Neck Injuries
Felipe Munera, MD, Gary Danton, MD, PhD, Luis A. Rivas, MD, Robert P. Henry, MD, and
Michelle G. Ferrari, MD
Penetrating neck trauma may occur from gun shots, stabbings, and accidental injury.
Approximately 50% of gunshot and 10%-20% of stab-wound patients are reported as
having severe injuries and the mortality from severe vascular injuries is reported as high as
50%. Penetrating traumatic neck injury is no longer best managed with exploratory surgery
or conventional angiography in the stable patient. Computed tomography angiography has
proven to be a useful, safe, and reliable means of diagnosis. Experience with interpreting
and reconstructing computed tomography images, understanding the clinically important
findings, and avoiding pitfalls is critical for successful patient care. Therefore, radiologists
and clinicians who treat trauma patients need to familiarize themselves with the computed
tomography angiography technique and appearances of vascular injury and know when to
recommend further evaluation, such as interventional angiography or open surgery.

Managing penetrating neck trauma in the acute setting has
been fraught with controversy. Between World War I and
World War II, the preferred treatment changed from conservative observation to surgical exploration in an effort to decrease
mortality rates. This held through the Vietnam era5 but as imaging technologies permitted less invasive evaluations the standard of care has slowly been changing to favor diagnostic imaging in hemodynamically stable patients.1 While ultrasound and
magnetic resonance angiography (MRA) have been studied in
this setting, computed tomography angiography (CTA) is currently the most widely used and accepted technique. At most
institutions CTA has replaced digital subtraction angiography as
the initial study of choice for the evaluation of the neck vessels.
Experience with interpreting and reconstructing computed tomographic (CT) images, understanding the clinically important

Anatomical Considerations
The neck is subdivided into 3 anatomic zones based on access to underlying anatomy. Zone 1 encompasses the region
from the clavicles and sternal notch to the cricothyroid membrane and is treated similarly to a thoracic injury. Zone 2
includes the area between the cricothyroid membrane and
the angle of the mandible. Zone 3 includes the area from the
angle of the mandible to the skull base (Fig. 1).6 Making
clinical decisions based on these anatomic zones is problematic because an external wound to a particular zone may not
reflect the trajectory of the knife or bullet. The zonal schema,
however, is still referenced in clinical discussions.7
The traditional spaces of the neck used in locating masses
have not found widespread utility in the trauma setting. Understanding the fascial planes may prove useful in locating
hematomas and metallic fragments that suggest injury to a
particular structure. The carotid space contains the carotid
artery, internal jugular vein, and nerves. Evidence of hemorrhage clearly within the carotid space warrants close clinical
follow-up and should raise the level of suspicion for the presence of a vascular injury. The retropharyngeal space is a
potential space posterior to the pharynx, anterior to the prevertebral musculature, and is most important in the trauma
setting because a retropharyngeal carotid artery may be identified. This variant anatomy is important to recognize for
presurgical planning and as a warning to an aggressive
