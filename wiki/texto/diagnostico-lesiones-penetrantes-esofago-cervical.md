Management options for patients with penetrating
neck trauma include mandatory exploration and
selective observation [I-4]. Disagreement over
management plans, primarily concerns the patient
with minimal or no findings of airway, gastrointestinal, or vascular injuries. Airway injuries are fairly
obvious when clinically important. Arteriography
accurately excludes arterial injuries [5,6]. Esophageal injury remains a diagnostic dilemma as the
accuracy of tests to identify this injury is unknown.
The risk of missing an esophageal injury is often
used to justify mandatory exploration of all injuries
penetrating the platysma [I ,7].
This study was designed to prospectively evaluate
the accuracy of barium swallow and endoscopy in
detecting esophageal injury after penetrating cervical trauma.

One hundred eighteen patients with penetrating neck
wounds in zones II and III were evaluated at Parkland
Memorial Hospital, Dallas, Texas, from July 1981 to December 1984. There were 96 male patients and 22 female
patients with an average age of 28 years. Seventy-seven
patients (65 percent) had been stabbed and 41 (35 percent) had been shot. All patients had stable vital signs
without altered sensorium and a cervical wound in proximity to the esophagus. Patients with continued bleeding
consistent with major vascular injuries in the neck or
airway difficulty were taken directly to the operating
room and excluded from the study.

The results were reviewed to assess the accuracy of
endoscopy and barium swallow to diagnose an esophageal
injury. Each patient’s chart was reviewed at discharge and
at least 30 days after discharge. The sensitivity, specificity, and accuracy was calculated for each diagnostic test.
Sensitivity was expressed as the number of true-positive
results divided by the number of true-positive results plus
the number of false-negative results. Specificity was expressed as the number of true-negative results divided by
the number of true-negative results plus the number of
false-positive results. Accuracy was expressed as the number of true-positive and true-negative results divided by
the total number of patients.

There were no organ injuries at cervical exploration in 79 patients (67 percent). The remaining 39
patients (33 percent) had 40 injuries, including 10
esophageal, 2 pharyngeal, 4 laryngeal, 4 tracheal, 8
arterial, 6 venous, 3 peripheral nerve, and 3 spinal
cord injuries.
Symptoms of esophageal injury included dysphagia, hematemesis, hoarseness, and odynophagia.
Signs included subcutaneous air or crepitus, retropharyngeal air, retropharyngeal edema, hematoma,
deviated trachea, and pneumomediastinum. Seven
