Blunt Assault: ‘Million Dollar Baby’
PANTELIS HADJIZACHARIA, M.D., PETER RHEE, M.D., M.P.H., VIRAJ PANDIT, M.D.,
HASSAN AZIZ, M.D., DONALD GREEN, M.D., BELLAL JOSEPH, M.D.

From the Division of Trauma, Critical Care, and Acute Care Surgery,
University of Arizona, Tucson, Arizona
Contrasting reports exist in the literature regarding the management of patients with blunt assault to
the head, neck, and face and more importantly for clearing the cervical spine. The purpose of our
study was to determine the risk of cervical spine injuries after blunt assault to the head, neck, and face
and its association with other injuries. We performed a retrospective case review of all blunt assault
trauma admissions to the head, neck, and face at our Level I trauma center. We identified all patients
who were assaulted with hands and feet and blunt instruments. A total of 3286 patients with blunt
assault to the head, neck, and face were identified of whom 11 (0.003%) were found to have a cervical
spine fracture or cervical spine subluxation. None of the patients had a cervical spinal cord injury. The
11 patients composed our study population with a mean age of 39 6 7.8 years, 100 per cent were male,
and the mean Injury Severity Score was 12 6 7.9. Five (45%) patients required surgery for stabilization
of the cervical spine. Mortality was reported in only one patient who had a C7 transverse process
fracture. Cervical spine injury after blunt assault is rare but does occur and encompasses significant
injuries requiring surgical intervention. However, these injuries are the result of direct blows to the
cervical spine and we suggest that assaulted patients with no direct trauma to the neck do not require
an exhaustive evaluation of the cervical spine.

Life Support course teaches to assume a cervical spine
injury in patients with blunt injury to the head, face,
and neck and mandates an elaborate cervical spine
examination.1 Multiple contrasting reports exist in the
literature for the management of these patients with
blunt assault to the head, neck, and face and more
importantly for clearing the cervical spine.2, 3 However, most of these studies have failed to distinguish
between the various mechanisms of blunt assault with
most of their patient group composed of motor vehicular injuries. The pattern of cervical spine injury
occurring in a patient after a blunt assault varies depending
on the mechanism of the assault. Motor vehicular accidents cause a flexion–extension pattern of cervical injury,
whereas a direct blow to the neck causes an impact injury.4
A direct blow to the neck produces more serious injuries
compared with an indirect force to the head, neck, or the
face as a result of the difference in kinematics of force.5
The primary aim of our study was to determine the risk of
cervical spine injuries after blunt assault to the head, neck,

spine evaluation for all blunt assault cases to the head,
neck, and face.
Methods

We performed a 14-year retrospective case review
of all blunt assault trauma admissions to the head,
neck, and face presenting to our academic Level I
trauma center. All patients who were assaulted with
hands, feet, and blunt instruments were identified
through our trauma registry using E codes (973,
968.2, 960, 805.0, 805.1, 806.0, 806.1, and 952).
Patients with a cervical spine injury or a cervical
column injury were identified and a chart review of
these patients was performed.
Patient variables were collected using Microsoft
Excel 2003 (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA),
which included demographics, mechanism of injury
(hands and feet vs blunt object), injury sustained
identified by International Classification of Diseases, 9th Revision codes, admission vitals, Glasgow
Coma Scale (GCS), Abbreviated Injury Score, Injury Severity Score (ISS), and outcomes. Statistical
analysis was performed using SAS System, Version
8.2 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC).

Results

with blunt assault to the head, face, and neck were
identified of whom 2286 (70.0%) had both head

patients had a cervical spinal cord injury. In these
patients, the mean age was 39 ± 7.8 years, 100 per cent
were male, and the mean ISS was 12 ± 7.9 (Table 1).

DISCUSSION

In our study, we demonstrated that blunt traumatic
assault with hands, feet, or a blunt object can be
a significant mechanism of injury for development of
cervical spine injury and other associated injuries. In
our study, three of the four patients who were assaulted
with a baseball bat required surgical intervention. One
patient required an ORIF with bone graft and halo vest
immobilization, two patients required halo vest immobilization, whereas one patient was treated with a cervical collar. Of the two patients who were punched and
kicked, one did not require treatment of cervical spine
injuries, although he did present with a left upper extremity radiculopathy associated with chronic cervical
vertebral disk changes. The second patient was assaulted
with a fist and fell, striking his occiput against a

The patient with an undetermined mechanism of assault did not require any therapeutic intervention for
the cervical spine, whereas the patient who was struck
with a metal pipe had multiple stable fractures of his
cervical spine and was discharged without any treatment for these injuries. There was only a single
reported mortality in a patient who sustained a C7
transverse process fracture, which did not require
treatment. This patient had extensive associated injuries including subdural and subarachnoid hemorrhage and he developed multiple organ failure, which
ultimately led to cardiopulmonary arrest and death.
Our study reproduces the finding of multiple papers
that have found a very low incidence of cervical spine
injury from blunt assault.2, 3, 5, 6 A study by Patton
et al. of 102 patients with blunt assault to the head and
neck found only one patient with a stable spinous
process fracture, who was discharged the next day. They
found no clinically significant cervical spine injuries
and concluded that victims of blunt assault, regardless
of neurological status, can be cleared on the basis of
plain radiographic evidence alone.7 In contrast, our
study shows that there were five patients requiring
surgical treatment after their beating (two patients had
an ORIF, bone graft, and halo; two had a halo; and one
had an ORIF and bone graft only). In addition, two of
the patients presented with neurological findings of
upper extremity radiculopathy.
It is intuitive that blows from a blunt instrument like
a baseball bat or a metal pipe will produce injuries of
greater severity than punches and kicks. Studies have
demonstrated that a baseball bat when swung full
swing can produce enough force to crush a human
skull,8 whereas kicks or punches produce comparably
lesser forces.9, 10 Surprisingly, a study by Shepherd
et al. reported a significantly higher incidence of fractures in attacks with fists and feet compared with attacks
with blunt weapons. They attributed this finding to the
greater momentum of fists and feet as compared with
most blunt objects.11 In our study, we found that patients
assaulted with a blunt object sustained serious injuries
requiring surgical management of the cervical spine
compared with patients assaulted with punches and
kicks. All patients in our current series sustained CSFS
from direct blows to their necks. According to Walicko
et al., a greater force is required to cause injury a cervical spine than that conveyed by a punch.9 One patient

Conclusions

Although the incidence of cervical spine fracture or
cervical spine subluxation from blunt assault is exceedingly rare, and no injuries to the cervical spinal
cord were identified, a small percentage of patients
sustained significant cervical spinal column injuries
requiring therapy, as shown in our 11 patients. It is
apparent that the transmission of biomechanical forces
to the cervical spine varies widely among blunt mechanisms such as motor vehicle collisions, fall from a
height, and assault. Prospective studies are needed to
establish guidelines for a more directed evaluation of

the cervical spine in patients sustaining blunt assault. As
described earlier, it is the direct blows to the neck and
not the face and head that result in significant cervical
spine injuries and we suggest that patients with this
mechanism of injury warrant a more thorough evaluation of their cervical spine.
