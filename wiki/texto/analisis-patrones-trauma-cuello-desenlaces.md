Abstract
Traumatic neck injuries (TNIs) constitute 5–10% of all trauma cases. Though the neck injuries are not frequent but the morbidity and mortality rates associated with these injuries are high. It was a single centre, ambispective observational study.
One hundred fifty-six Patients with TNI of either sex > 14 years of age and giving an informed consent by self or their legally
authorized representative getting admitted under trauma surgery were included in study. On comparing retrospective Vs
prospective cohort penetrating injury was more common than blunt injury but there is increase in the rate of blunt injury
i.e., from 3.2 to 17.7%. In retrospective cohort most common mechanism was assault (46.6%) followed by road traffic injury
(26%). While in the prospective cohort the most common mechanism was road traffic injury (22.6%) followed by assault
(22%). PNI (Penetrating neck injury) was the most common injury pattern predominantly following assault or self-inflicted
harm. But in the recent times there was a change in the trend toward blunt neck injury pattern.

Introduction
Traumatic injury is a major health problem affecting mainly
the young productive youth worldwide. Trauma related morbidity and mortality is still high (1). Traumatic neck injuries
(TNIs) constitute 5%–10% of all trauma cases (1). Though
the neck injuries are not frequent but the morbidity and
mortality rates associated with these injuries are high (2).
They can be potentially life-threatening due to the involvement of the aerodigestive tract and major neck vessels that
are relatively unprotected. Hence a timely resuscitation and
intervention with a multidisciplinary team approach is critical for the successful outcome of these patients.
Literatures have broadly classified TNI into two groups
i.e., penetrating and blunt neck injury primarily based on the
mechanism of the injury. Although the incidence of penetrating neck injuries predominates over the blunt, but in recent
decades, the incidence of injuries following blunt impact
has increased, primarily attributed due to the road traffic
injuries (3, 4).

Management of traumatic neck injuries pose a surgical
challenge because multiple vital structures are vulnerable to
injuries in the small, confined unprotected area (5). Priorities
for treating patients with neck injuries are the same as for
any other acutely injured patients following trauma primarily based on the standard trauma management protocol i.e.,
the Advanced Trauma life support (ATLS) of the American
College of Surgeons. Majority of the literatures on TNI are
from western developed world and only a few literatures
if any exists, addresses the issues in the low- and middleincome countries, inhabited by more than two third of the
world’s population.

The peak age of occurrence was noted in the age group
of 20–45 years. In comparing retrospective vs prospective
cohort data there were no significant differences noted in
mean age and gender involvement. Our result was in concordance with most studies. AI-Thani et al. reported the
mean age was 31 ± 9 years and a majority (98%) was males
(3). Zakaria et al. too have reported that in patients with TNI
males outnumbered females (i.e., 87.4%vs13.6%) and the
average age was 36 years(5).
In the present study, the most common type of injury was
penetrating injury noted in 91.0% cases while blunt injury
was noted in only 9.0% cases. This observation of the study
was in concordance with the result reported by Irish et al. in
their study where 78% patients had a penetrating neck injury
while 22% patients had blunt neck injury (6). In contradictory to this, Farner et al. have reported that the majority of
patients with TNI had sustained blunt neck injuries (65.5%)
(7).
Studies have highlighted that blunt neck injury is about
5% of all traumatic neck injuries, mainly following motor
vehicle crash. The kinematics of the injury is due to the
sudden acceleration-deceleration mechanism which results
in the crushing of the trachea or compression of the oesophagus against the cervical spine (3). In our study, we observed
blunt neck injury was noted in about 9% of all traumatic
neck injuries and in 43% cases it was following RTI. The
possible reason could be the increased number of trauma
cases following MVC due to RTI. Majority of (90%) road
fatalities worldwide are from low and middle-income countries. Pedestrians, cyclists, and motorcycle riders are considered "vulnerable road users" and account for 50% of all
road fatalities worldwide. There is a need of strict legislation
in India to address all five behavioural risk factors (speed,
drink-driving, helmets, seat-belts, and child restraints) and
decrease the risk of trauma following RTI (8).
In the present study, the predominant mechanism in TNI
was an assault in 32.7% patients followed by in decreasing
order RTI in 24.4%, self-inflicted injury in 21.1%, accidental in 12.2%, manja injury in 2.6%. High velocity gunshot
injury was noted in 2.6% cases. However, when the data
form the both the cohort was analysed, it was noted that
in the retrospective cohort the most common mechanism
was assault followed by road traffic injury and self-inflicted
injury, but in prospective data analysis, road traffic injury
was observed as the most common cause followed by assault
and self-inflicted injury at the same frequency. This result
was in concordance with the result reported by Zakaria et al.
in their study where assault was the most common cause
of injury in 55.2% cases, followed by deliberate self-harm
(DSH) in 38% and accidental injury in 6.8% cases (5).
A zone I and zone III incidence in neck injuries were
13.5 and 1%, respectively in this study, as opposed to 15 and
24% and 14 and 18% in previously published data (9–11).

Conclusion

time of initial presentation had good outcome if diagnosed
and managed timely.
