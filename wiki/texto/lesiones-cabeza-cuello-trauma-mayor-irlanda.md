Head and neck injury in major trauma in Ireland: a multicentre
retrospective analysis of patterns and surgical workload
Abdulrahman Mohamed 1

2

& Jeffrey Mulcaire & Anthony James P. Clover

1,3

Received: 3 June 2020 / Accepted: 2 July 2020 / Published online: 9 July 2020
# Royal Academy of Medicine in Ireland 2020

Abstract
Purpose Head and neck injuries are common in major trauma patients, but the prevalence is poorly documented in the literature.
This study aims to investigate this further, particularly in the context of increasing development of Irish and European trauma
systems.
Aims To determine the prevalence of, and patterns behind head and neck injury in major trauma in an Irish population.
Materials and methods The Trauma Audit Research Network database was analysed for major trauma patients (injury severity
score > 15) with head and neck injuries admitted to any of the 26 participating hospitals in the Republic of Ireland between 2014
and 2017. The data was studied for patterns in the epidemiology of injuries and outcomes.
Results A total of 5364 patients were identified. Males were affected more than females (M:F 2.1:1).
The most common mechanism of injury was falls. There were 1272 counts of soft tissue injury, and 5814 counts of bony
injury recorded within the cohort. Twenty-six percent of patients underwent some form of surgical procedure. Median length of
hospital stay was 8 days, and the 30-day survival rate was 90%.
Conclusions A substantial number of Irish major trauma patients of all ages and genders sustain some form of head and neck
injury. It produces significant injury and surgical workload involving input from multiple disciplines.
Keywords Epidemiology . Facial injuries . Head injuries . Multiple trauma . Western Europe

estimated 5.8 million people globally die from it and many

Trauma of the head and neck comprises fracture of the
facial bones, soft tissue injuries of the face and neck and
dentoalveolar injuries [3]. A considerable number of injured
patients sustain some form of head and neck trauma, either in
isolation or in combination with other injuries [4]. Major trauma is any trauma that may cause death or severe disability. It
can refer to a single injury or multiple injuries to the same or
different body regions and systems [5]. Whether or not an
injury is a major trauma can be determined using the injury
severity score (ISS) [6]. An ISS of ≥ 16 is the cutoff for major
trauma, and this is calculated by summing up the scores of the
three most severe injuries a patient sustains. The management
of head and neck trauma can be uniquely complex as it often
requires input from a wide variety of surgical disciplines including maxillofacial surgery, plastic surgery and neurosurgery [7, 8].
Although head and neck injury in the general population
has been well-studied to date [9–11], the literature has been
relatively sparse with studies on it in a major trauma population [12, 13], and none in the Irish population.

Materials and methods
The TARN database was searched for major trauma patients
with head and neck injuries admitted to any of the 26 participating hospitals in the Republic of Ireland between 2014 and
2017 (inclusive).
The TARN database collects data on over 100 variables
relating to the patient, their injury and hospital stay. For the
purposes of this study, a selection of relevant variables was
chosen for analysis and was divided into two groups:
The first included sociodemographic measures relating to
the patient cohort as well as the circumstances and factors
relating to their injuries, management and outcomes. These
included age, sex, injury location, evidence of intoxication
on admission, and injury intent.
The second group consisted of clinical variables. These
were the mechanism of injury, whether it was blunt or penetrating, the anatomical location of the injuries themselves (further subdivided into soft tissue and hard tissue injuries), injury
severity, treatment received, length of stay in hospital, and 30day mortality rates.
Descriptive analysis was performed using SPSS (IBM
SPSS® Statistics for Macintosh, Version 26).
This study received ethical approval from the Clinical
Research Ethics Committee of University College Cork.

Results

5364 (27.5%) sustained some form of head, face and/or neck
injury.

Age and sex
The mean age of the sample was 53.57 years (± 25.3 SD,
range 0.05–102.4). However, 2155 patients (40%) were over
the age of 65. Figure 1 shows this distribution in deciles. The
70–80 decile had the highest number of patients at 879 (16%).
Within the sample, 68% of patients were male (3653) and
32% were female (1711) giving a male:female ratio of
2.125:1.

Injury location
The home by far is the most frequent place of injury, comprising 43% (2322) of the sample. Roads and public areas were
the next most frequent, at 22% (1177) and 20% (1079) respectively. In 119 cases (2%), the injury location was not known.
The office was the least common place to be injured, with only
4 patients (< 1%). Other locations of injuries included tube
tracks and rail tracks. This data is clarified in Table 1.

Intoxication
Evidence of intoxication was described in 1602 cases
(29.8%). Of these, 95% were alcohol-related, with the remaining 5% (95) cases being related to drugs, or both alcohol and
drugs.

their injuries non-intentionally. Suspected self-harm or where
the intent was inconclusive represented a minority of cases,

Mechanism and nature of injury
Falls of some description were the most frequent aetiology
leading to injury in the cohort, accounting for approximately
two-thirds of cases (3558 patients). The least common causes
included stabbings/shootings, crush injuries, burns and blasts,
each accounting for less than 1%. Table 3 illustrates the different aetiologies of the trauma sustained by the cohort as a whole.
The major aetiology of injury did vary somewhat with age.
This is depicted in Fig. 2 with the sample being divided into 3
age groups. The most common aetiology remained falls in the
35–70 and > 70 groups. However, in the 0–35 age group, road
traffic accidents (RTAs) were the most frequent aetiology,
decreasing in frequency with increasing age. While blows
represented 10% (552) of the whole sample, over half of these
were among patients in the 0–35 group (297). Ninety-nine
percent of injuries were blunt in nature (5289), with 1% (75)
being penetrating.

Outcomes

Major trauma places a high burden on health care systems
[15]. Head and neck injury in particular often results in complex injuries which in turn produce both considerable financial
[16] and personal impact [17].
The present study was carried out to establish the prevalence of head, face and neck injury in the setting of an Irish

major trauma population. It sought to examine the
sociodemographic (age, sex, etc.) and clinical factors (injury
types, treatments) underlying it. The data were extracted from
one of Europe’s largest major trauma databases, the TARN
database.
Data from 26 participating hospitals across the republic of
Ireland between January 2014 and December 2017 were
analysed producing a study population of 3653 males and
1711 females.
Out of the 19,483 major trauma patients included in the
TARN database during the study period, 5364 sustained some
of form of head and neck injury, giving a prevalence of
27.5%. This is higher than that reported in other studies carried out with comparable methodologies and over similar time
frames which showed rates of 16% [12] and 17% [13].
However, these studies included maxillofacial injuries only, and therefore may not have included patients with head or

Many previous studies have demonstrated that men tend to
be the victims of head, face or neck trauma more commonly
than women [9, 10, 12, 13, 18–24]. This finding can also be
seen in the present study which shows that major trauma patients with head, face or neck injuries were more likely to be
male (M:F 2.125:1).
There are several hypotheses to explain this pattern including cultural attitudes towards occupation [20], social activities
[9], hazardous driving [25] and the urban location of major
trauma centres [22]. Though the reason(s) in the case of our
study is unclear, it is interesting to note that the pattern
persists.
Trauma has been shown to exert a significant health burden
on all populations. It has however been traditionally viewed as
a disease of the young, and in particular, of males involved in
RTAs and interpersonal violence [26]. In recent times, major
trauma in the elderly has become an increasingly recognised
and studied phenomenon [27]. This is in line with our study
finding that the mean age of injury was in the 5th decade. This
is considerably higher than the age patterns demonstrated in
the literature on craniofacial and neck injury which demonstrate mean ages ranging from 25 to 39 [9, 10, 18–23].
In many countries, the elderly comprise the most rapidly
expanding section of the population. Indeed in Ireland, the
increase in the number of persons over the age of 65 is approaching 20,000 per year [28]. This increase in the proportion of the population that is elderly, combined with increased
life expectancy and activity levels all serve to explain the
increasing numbers of elderly patients suffering major trauma.

This finding has important implications. Head, face and
neck injuries are inherently associated with considerable morbidity. This is further worsened in the elderly owing to preexisting disease, increasing frailty with age and slower recovery [29].
In our study, intoxication of some form was found in almost 30% of cases, with the most commonly implicated agent
being alcohol. Furthermore, our study showed that the vast
majority of injuries (84%) were non-intentional. There is a
well-established link between alcohol and many types of unintentional injuries, RTAs, falls, burns and occupational injuries [30]. The link between alcohol consumption and facial
injury specifically has also been documented in the literature.
A 1998 UK survey found that at least 22% of all facial injuries
were associated with alcohol consumption [11].
This underscores the continuingly important role of addressing alcohol consumption in the prevention of these
injuries.
Historically, RTAs were the most common causes of both
major trauma and craniofacial injury. This can be seen in
studies carried out before the advent of modern advancements
in vehicle safety, where up to 70% of injuries were caused by
RTAs [13].
In recent times, however, there has been a significant shift
in the aetiology of head and neck injury towards falls, and in
particular, ‘low falls,’ i.e. those less than 2 m [24, 27]. Our
study showed that falls were the most frequent cause of injury
in almost two-thirds of cases, with low falls accounting for
over half of all cases.
This pattern was seen in all age groups but the 0–35 group,
where it was the second most common cause (Fig. 2).
This is supported by the finding that almost twice the number of injuries occurred at home (2322), compared with that on
the roadside (1177). A large proportion of injuries due to falls
occur in the elderly, and many occur during daily activities
[31]. Consideration should therefore be given both to better
home design to prevent this, as well as more widespread use of
fall prevention strategies such as those outlined by Kannus
et al. [32].
The causes of head and neck injury in this study were
diverse, as were the resultant injuries. There was a total of
1272 counts of soft tissue injury, most frequently affecting
the scalp or the face, and 5814 counts of bony fractures, most

commonly affecting the skull. The literature is conflicting
with regard to the most commonly injured structures in the
head and neck. Many studies on both major and non-major
trauma reported the mandible as the most commonly injured
structure [9, 20, 21], while in the others, it was the midface
[13, 18]. Direct comparison of injury sites is complicated by
the heterogeneity in anatomical areas included for analysis by
studies in the literature.
Within the sample, head injuries tended to be both more
prevalent and more severe than facial injuries, and this pattern
was consistent across different age groups and genders.
Our study had an operative intervention rate of 26%
(1402). This value is lower than the 43% seen in major trauma
patients reported in the 2018 Irish Major Trauma Audit
(MTA) [2] but higher than that seen in major trauma patients
when considering maxillofacial injury alone [12].
Median length of stay in hospital in our study was 8 days
with 90% of patients being discharged within a month.
The mortality rate in our study was 10%, twice the
overall value reported in the MTA [2]. This is most probably due to the higher likelihood of injury to the brain and
other critical structures that accompany a head and neck
injury.

Conclusions
Head, face and neck injury occurs in over 1 in 4 Irish major
trauma patients. It affects patients of all ages and sexes and
produces considerable injury and surgical workload.
This study provides the first detailed description of the
patterns head and neck injury in an Irish major trauma population. Its findings are reflective of a shift in the
sociodemographic and aetiologic landscape of major trauma
in that the elderly are increasingly affected with more and
more cases attributable to falls.
At a time where the Irish trauma system is seeing noteworthy advancements in its development, such as the release of
the Major Trauma Audit, understanding the aetiology and
epidemiology of these injuries is a crucial step in informing
targeted preventative measures to reduce trauma burden. It
also allows for efficient planning of care for these patients,
and ensuring that this care meets both best-practice criteria
as well as their needs.
